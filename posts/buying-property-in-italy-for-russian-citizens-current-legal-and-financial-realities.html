Buying property in Italy for Russian citizens: current legal and financial realities

KrypticKnight
Topic Starter
Posted: 25.01.2025 03:43
I am a Russian citizen currently exploring the possibility of purchasing a residential property in Italy for investment and vacation purposes. Given the recent geopolitical situation and banking restrictions, I am struggling to understand the current procedure for transferring funds and verifying the legality of the transaction. Could anyone share their recent experience or provide advice on which banks are still facilitating cross-border payments for such purposes? I would also appreciate recommendations for reliable real estate lawyers who specialize in assisting Russian clients with the 'codice fiscale' and residency documentation.

Replies (15)

PhantomPhreak
Replied: 25.01.2025 13:52
The current landscape is extremely complex. Most major Italian banks refuse transfers from Russian banks due to SWIFT restrictions. You will likely need to use a third-party country like Kazakhstan or Serbia as an intermediary for your funds.
ApexAlchemist
Replied: 27.02.2025 12:40
I recently went through this process. You definitely need a lawyer who understands the 'Anti-Money Laundering' (AML) requirements, as Italian banks are under intense scrutiny regarding Russian capital. It is not impossible, but it requires a lot of documentation.
RustRider
Replied: 20.03.2025 11:02
Getting a 'codice fiscale' is still possible for Russian citizens at the Italian consulate, but the main hurdle is proving the source of funds. Make sure your bank statements are notarized and translated by a certified professional.

ObsidianOracle
Replied: 19.04.2025 11:57
Don't even try to transfer directly from a Russian bank account. Many people are using crypto-to-fiat services, but be warned: Italian notaries are very strict about the 'provenance of funds' and might reject the transaction if the money trail isn't perfectly clean.
GlitchGourmet

I recommend contacting a boutique law firm in Milan or Rome. They usually have experience with non-EU clients. Expect to pay a premium for the extra due diligence they have to perform on your behalf.

I had to open an account in a neutral country to bridge the payment. It added six months to the process, but the notary finally accepted the funds once they originated from an EU-compliant financial institution.

The 'codice fiscale' part is actually the easiest. It is the banking compliance that will keep you up at night. Do not sign a 'proposta d'acquisto' until you have a bank that has pre-cleared your source of funds.
